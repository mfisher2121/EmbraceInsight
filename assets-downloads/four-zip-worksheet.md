# The Four-ZIP Quick-Win Worksheet

## A seven-day plan for one useful local marketing experiment

For HVAC owners and small marketing teams. A Contractor Playbook draft from Embrace Insight; confirm attribution before release.

**The idea:** Pick four ZIP codes you can serve reliably. Identify one customer question. Improve one touchpoint. Track what happens before doing more.

Four ZIP codes create a manageable planning boundary. They are not a special search-ranking signal or a promise of growth. ZIP codes may cross municipal, utility, or program boundaries; verify actual service addresses separately.

## 1. Choose your four ZIP codes

Start with places where travel, scheduling, licensing, and service support make sense. Use your own job records and customer knowledge; do not infer household income, needs, or incentive eligibility from ZIP code alone.

## 2. Find a question worth answering

Review recent inquiries and ask your service team what customers misunderstand. Avoid putting customer names, addresses, or account details in this worksheet.

Example topic: “What information should I gather before asking for a heat pump estimate?” This is a suggested topic, not a finding about your customers.

## 3. Improve one customer touchpoint

Choose a relevant existing service page, estimate-request page, or follow-up email. Avoid creating nearly identical ZIP pages just to target search terms.

- [ ] State the real service and service area clearly.
- [ ] Answer the chosen question in plain language.
- [ ] Explain what a customer should prepare and what happens next.
- [ ] Use a working call, email, or request form.
- [ ] Confirm the page works on a phone.
- [ ] Remove unsupported savings, ranking, rebate, and response-time claims.
- [ ] Use only photos and testimonials you have permission to publish.
- [ ] Keep business details consistent with your actual operation.

**Suggested copy pattern:** “Considering `[SERVICE]` in `[ACTUAL SERVICE AREA]`? Before requesting an estimate, gather `[USEFUL DETAILS]`. Our next step is `[TRUE PROCESS]`. Request `[ACTUAL OFFER]` at `[WORKING CONTACT]`.”

Don't call an estimate free or promise a response deadline unless your business honors that offer.

## 4. Run the seven-day work plan

| Day | Action | Done when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Select four ZIP codes and check capacity | The table above has a reason for each choice |
| 2 | Review recent customer questions | One question and a supported answer are selected |
| 3 | Update one useful touchpoint | The copy accurately reflects your service |
| 4 | Test the customer path | A test inquiry reaches the correct person |
| 5 | Share with an appropriate existing audience | Permission and channel rules have been checked |
| 6 | Review inquiries and team feedback | Questions and source information are logged |
| 7 | Decide what to keep or change | A next action and review date are recorded |

Seven days is a schedule for completing the work, not a deadline for acquiring customers. Use a longer observation window where volume is low.

*Define a qualified inquiry before starting: a real service request within your service area and offered services, excluding spam, duplicates, and test submissions. Adapt the definition to your business and use it consistently. Keep identifying details in your approved customer system, not a shared marketing worksheet.*

Count inquiries, qualified inquiries, appointments, and completed jobs separately. “Appointments ÷ qualified inquiries” is an appointment rate only when the cohort and observation window match; if the denominator is zero, report “not available.” A before/after change alone does not establish that the experiment caused it.
