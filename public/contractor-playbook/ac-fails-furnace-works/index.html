When the AC fails and the furnace still works, how to explain the three options

By Max Fisher · Playbook guide · 2026-11-07

For owners, comfort advisors and service techs · Montgomery County, Maryland · Figures from the five-ZIP permit report

The short version. Name all three options, in the same order, every time: replace the AC, put in a heat pump that works with the furnace, or switch fully to a heat pump. For each one, say what it replaces, what it keeps, and what you have to check before you can quote it. Then let the homeowner choose.

Why this call matters here

In our five-ZIP permit report, 153 residential replacement permits over the past year named an AC and no heating equipment, and 65% of them were issued April through July. Census estimates say utility gas heats about 66% of occupied homes in those ZIPs. Put together, that’s the common call: the AC quit in the heat, and a gas furnace that still runs sits in the basement.

It’s also the call where the homeowner has the least time to think. If only one option comes up, it’s usually the AC. Laying out all three takes a few extra minutes, and the homeowner leaves knowing what they chose and why.

Ask three questions before any price

  1. How old is the furnace, and has it given trouble? Read the data plate. Don’t guess.
  2. How long do they plan to stay in the house?
  3. What bothers them now? A hot or cold room, the gas bill, noise, a remodel coming up.

The answers tell you which option to describe in the most detail. You still describe all three.

How to say it

Keep it short and keep the order. Something like this:

“Your AC needs replacing, and your furnace still works. So you have three ways to go. One: we replace just the AC, and you keep heating with gas like you do today. Two: we put in a heat pump instead of an AC. It cools the same way, and it also heats the house for most of the winter, with your furnace taking over when it’s coldest. Three: we replace both with a heat pump system and retire the furnace. I’ll write down what each one includes, what I’d need to check, and the price, and you decide.”

Put it in writing

One page, three columns, the same order you used out loud. For each option, list:

  • The equipment and its size
  • What stays in place
  • What has to be checked or upgraded first, such as the panel, the ducts or the controls
  • The price, the warranty and how long the work takes

If you mention a rebate, write the program’s name and the date you checked it. Program rules and deadlines change.

What not to do

  • Don’t present one option and bring up the others only if you’re asked.
  • Don’t promise a savings number you can’t show the math for. Operating cost depends on gas and electric rates, the equipment, the house and how the homeowner runs it.
  • Don’t call the furnace unsafe without a test result to show.
  • Don’t size the new equipment by the old unit’s tonnage alone.

What we don’t know yet

We don’t yet know how often local homeowners pick each option, or what tips the decision. The permits show the result in only some of the jobs: 25 use hybrid or dual-fuel wording, 80 name a heat pump and a furnace or boiler together, and 3 spell out a full switch.
