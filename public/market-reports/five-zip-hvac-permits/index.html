MARKET REPORT / FIVE ZIPS / MECHANICAL PERMITS

What a year of HVAC permits tells us about replacements in five Montgomery County ZIPs

By Max Fisher · Market report · 2026-11-07

Montgomery County mechanical permits issued 2025-11-07 through 2026-10-28 · ZIPs 20814, 20815, 20816, 20817 and 20895 · County data retrieved 2026-11-07

The finding. Over the year, the county issued 1,175 mechanical replacement permits for single-family homes and townhouses in these five ZIPs. 939 of them say what equipment went in. Of those, 277 (29%) name a heat pump, and 319 (34%) name a furnace or boiler with no heat pump. Just 3 spell out a switch from a gas furnace to a heat pump. Census estimates say utility gas heats about 66% of occupied homes in these ZIPs.

Four things stand out.

  • A furnace and an AC together is the most common job that names its equipment, with 189 permits. Another 130 name a furnace or boiler with no AC.
  • Heat pumps appear in four forms. 169 name a heat pump with no furnace or boiler, 80 name a heat pump and a furnace or boiler together, 25 use hybrid or dual-fuel wording, and 3 describe a switch away from a gas furnace. A further 32 name ductless or mini-split equipment.
  • 153 permits name an AC and no heating equipment. These are the jobs where the cooling side was replaced and the heating side, as far as the permit says, was left alone.
  • 236 permits (20%) carry no equipment detail at all. The description is blank, or holds only a form phrase or a reference number. Any share in this report could move if those permits were described.

When the work happened

The season shows up in the descriptions. 99 of the 153 AC-only replacements (65%) were issued April through July. Furnace-only replacements ran the other way: 95 of 130 (73%) were issued November through March.

How homes in these ZIPs heat

The Census Bureau’s American Community Survey asks households what fuel heats their home. Across these five ZIPs, an estimated 36,043 of 54,892 occupied homes (66%) heat with utility gas, and 17,126 (31%) with electricity. The gas share runs from 55% in 20814 to 75% in 20895.

Read the two sources together with care. The Census figures cover every occupied home, including apartments and condominiums, while the permits in this report are houses and townhouses. The estimates are a 2020 to 2024 average, not a 2026 count. Electric heat in a home can mean a heat pump or electric resistance heat; the survey doesn’t say which.

What the permits can’t tell us

  • Why the work happened. A permit doesn’t say whether the old system failed, the owner planned an upgrade, or the house was being remodeled or sold.
  • What the homeowner was offered. We see what was permitted, not the options that were quoted and turned down.
  • Price, financing or rebates. None of these are in the county data.
  • Whether a heat pump and a furnace on one permit are one system. Some of those 80 descriptions are a dual-fuel setup; others describe two separate systems in one house, such as an attic heat pump and a basement furnace.
  • Whether the house kept its gas service.
  • How many homes this is. One house can have more than one permit, and one permit can cover several systems.
  • Work done without a permit. It doesn’t appear here.

This report also leaves out lead costs, close rates and comparisons between named companies. We’ll publish those only when we have data we can cite.

What we still need to learn

The permits show what was installed. The next part has to come from the people involved.

From contractors: When the AC fails and the furnace still works, which options do you put in front of the homeowner? How often is each one chosen? What rules out a heat pump most often: the electrical panel, the price, the furnace’s age, or worry about comfort in the cold?

From homeowners: What were you offered? What did you choose, and why? Did anyone lay the options out in writing?

If you can answer any of these, write to max@maxfisher.net. When we have enough answers to report, we’ll publish them as counts, with the number of responses and the gaps in who answered.

Method

We downloaded every record in the county’s Mechanical Permits dataset with an issue date from 2025-11-07 through 2026-10-28 and one of the five ZIP codes: 1,973 permits. Our download matched the county’s own count for each ZIP. 1,550 are for single-family dwellings or townhouses. Of those, 1,175 have the work type “Replace” and are the subject of this report. The other 375 have the work type “Install,” which takes in new houses and additions as well as equipment, so we kept them out of the replacement counts.

Each description is lowercased and checked against these rows in order. The first match wins:

  1. No equipment detail in the description
  2. Explicit gas, oil or propane to heat pump switch
  3. Hybrid or dual-fuel wording
  4. Heat pump and furnace or boiler both named
  5. Ductless or mini-split named
  6. Heat pump named, no furnace or boiler
  7. Furnace or boiler and AC both named
  8. Furnace or boiler named, no AC or heat pump
  9. AC named, no heating equipment
  10. Not heating or cooling equipment (water heaters, fireplaces, exhaust, gas piping)
  11. Ductwork named, no equipment type
  12. HVAC work named, equipment type unclear
  13. Other wording

The rules are simple keyword matches, so they can be checked by hand. We read samples from every row to test them; typos and unusual wording will still put some permits in the wrong row. The classification script is part of this publication’s source files.

An earlier classified file we worked from listed 1,542 permits for the same ZIPs and dates. We couldn’t reproduce which county records it left out, so this report starts again from the county’s full export.
